1. Problem Definition¶

Research question: Can pre-match information (recent form, head-to-head record, venue, and toss) predict the winner of an IPL match?

  • Target variable: a_wins — 1 if randomly assigned Team A won, 0 if Team B won.
  • Task type: binary classification.
  • Who could benefit: team analysts and coaches, broadcasters and journalists, and fans who want to know whether common pre-match beliefs hold up.
  • Why it matters: fans and commentators often treat form, home ground, and the toss as decisive. Testing these claims with data shows whether pre-match narratives are justified.

2. Background and Context¶

The IPL is a T20 league of 8–10 franchise teams. Before each match, captains toss a coin and the winner chooses to bat or field. T20 matches are short, so a single over can change the result, and squads change between seasons through player auctions.

The toss: A study of over 300 T20 internationals found little evidence that winning the toss or batting first improves the chance of winning Sacheti et al., 2016. A larger study of over 35,000 matches found the toss effect is essentially zero in T20s overall, but about 3.3 percentage points in day/night limited-overs matches (Lal et al., 2023).

Home advantage: In English one-day county cricket, home teams won 57% of decided matches, though team quality mattered more than the toss Morley & Thomas, 2005.

Previous IPL prediction: Srikantaiah et al. 2021 compared SVM, random forest, logistic regression, and KNN using player-level statistics and reported 88% accuracy with a random forest. This project uses only team-level pre-match features and tests on future seasons the model has never seen, giving a more conservative estimate.

Expectation: research suggests the toss and home advantage have small effects in T20, so recent form and head-to-head (measures of team strength) were expected to matter most.

3. Data Description¶

  • Source: IPL Complete Dataset (2008–2024) by Patrick B. on Kaggle, originally from Cricsheet: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/patrickb1912/ipl-complete-dataset-20082020
  • Each row: one IPL match.
  • Size: 1,095 matches, 20 columns, 17 seasons (2008–2024). 1,090 remain after removing no-result matches.
  • Target distribution: in the raw data, team1 was the home team in 52.6% of matches but team2 in only 11.7%, so column order was not random. After randomly assigning Team A, the target is balanced (Team A won 50.8%), so there is no class imbalance.
  • Available features: season, date, city, venue, team1, team2, toss winner, toss decision, winner, plus post-match columns (result margin, player of the match, target runs) that I excluded.
  • Limitations: no player lineups, injuries, pitch, or weather data; franchises renamed over time; some seasons played at neutral venues 2009 South Africa, 2020 UAE. Missing values: city (51), winner and player_of_match 5 each, the no-result matches, result_margin 19, target_runs and target_overs 3 each, and method 1,074, filled only for rain-affected matches.

4. Data Understanding and Exploration¶

  • Summary statistics: matches per season grew after 2022 when the league expanded to 10 teams.
  • Target distribution: in the raw data, team1 won X% of matches, so column order was not random. After randomly assigning Team A, the target is balanced (Team A won 50.8%), so there is no class imbalance.
  • Patterns: toss winners won about half of matches overall and only 45.1% in 2023–2024; captains increasingly chose to field first; long-run team win rates vary.
  • How it informed my choices: the weak toss effect meant I needed features measuring team strength, so I built recent form and head-to-head. The team1 imbalance led me to randomize Team A and Team B.

5. Data Preparation and Feature Selection¶

  • Missing/inconsistent data: removed no-result matches no winner; merged renamed franchises e.g., Delhi Daredevils → Delhi Capitals; standardized city names Bangalore → Bengaluru; converted date to datetime and created year.
  • Features included: recent form last 5 matches, head-to-head wins, home city indicator, toss winner, and toss decision. All were engineered using only matches before each game. The final model uses their Team A minus Team B differences.
  • Excluded: player_of_match, result, result_margin, target_runs, target_overs, super_over, and method leak the result; umpires are irrelevant.
  • Encoding/scaling: toss and home features coded 0/1; numeric features scaled with StandardScaler for logistic regression.
  • Split: train on 2008–2022 946 matches, test on 2023–2024 144 matches.
  • Leakage prevention: time-based split, features built only from earlier matches, scaling fitted inside a pipeline on training data only.

6. Baseline and Model Development¶

  • Baselines: majority class 48.6% and toss winner wins 45.1%. They show what accuracy is possible without a model.
  • Models: logistic regression interpretable, gives win probabilities, standard for binary outcomes and random forest captures nonlinear interactions, such as the toss mattering more at some venues.
  • Tuning: limited random forest depth to 5 to prevent overfitting, and compared two feature sets 9 raw features vs. 5 difference features using 5-fold time-series cross-validation.
  • Fair comparison: both models used identical training and test sets, the same features, and the same random seed 42.

7. Model Evaluation and Selection¶

  • Metrics: accuracy share of correct predictions and ROC-AUC how well the model ranks likely winners above likely losers; 0.5 = random. The classes are balanced and both errors are equally costly, so both metrics are appropriate.
  • Results: logistic regression 47.9% accuracy ROC-AUC 0.509; random forest 50.7% ROC-AUC 0.478. Neither beat the baselines.
  • Final model: random forest with difference features, which had the highest cross-validated ROC-AUC 0.524 ± 0.039. Final test: 46.5% accuracy, ROC-AUC 0.471.
  • Evidence and tradeoffs: all models scored 0.48–0.52 in cross-validation, so differences are within noise. The final choice favored fewer features over a meaningful performance gain.

8. Model Interpretation and Insights¶

  • What the model learned: almost nothing. The final model reached 46.5% test accuracy (ROC-AUC 0.471), equivalent to chance for 144 matches.
  • Confusion matrix: 67 correct and 77 incorrect predictions, spread evenly across both classes (32 correct Team B, 35 correct Team A, 42 false positives, 35 false negatives). The model does not favor either side; it is simply right about as often as it is wrong.
  • Feature importance: every feature's error bar crosses zero, so none reliably helps. Head-to-head and form difference were slightly negative, meaning patterns from 2008–2022 did not hold in 2023–2024, likely because squads change between seasons.
  • Form check: teams in clearly better recent form won 53.8% of matches, versus 51.0% for teams in clearly worse form and 49.9% when form was similar. These differences are within sampling noise (~200 matches per group), confirming the features are calculated correctly but are weak predictors.
  • Conclusions: these pre-match factors carry little predictive signal for IPL outcomes. The model cannot show that the toss, home ground, or form never matter, only that their effects are too small to detect in this data.

9. Limitations, Ethics, and Reflection¶

  • Biases and gaps: newer teams have little history; squads change after auctions, so old head-to-head records describe different players; neutral-venue seasons distort home advantage; no player, injury, or weather data.
  • Who is affected: bettors and fantasy players could lose money; teams and captains could face unfair media narratives.
  • Error consequences: since Team A is random, false positives and false negatives are the same mistake, picking the wrong winner. Confident wrong predictions are the most harmful because they create false certainty.
  • Real-world use: not appropriate for betting or team decisions, since it performs at chance level.
  • Next steps: player-level features from deliveries.csv, squad strength after each auction, and testing other form windows.
  • What users should know: these pre-match factors do not reliably predict IPL winners.

swap = rng.random(len(df)) < 0.5

def a_b(col1, col2):
    return np.where(swap, df[col2], df[col1]), np.where(swap, df[col1], df[col2])

model_df = pd.DataFrame(index=df.index)

model_df['a_form'], model_df['b_form'] = a_b('team1_form', 'team2_form')
model_df['a_prev_matches'], model_df['b_prev_matches'] = a_b('team1_prev_matches', 'team2_prev_matches')
model_df['a_home'], model_df['b_home'] = a_b('team1_home', 'team2_home')
a_h2h, b_h2h = a_b('team1_h2h_wins', 'team2_h2h_wins')
